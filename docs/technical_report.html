Baseline Batched Decode: Divergent vs. Greedy-Shared Trap

bench-batch · --max-mem 6 GiB

Running identical prompts with greedy decoding tricks every sequence into picking the exact same 8 of 128 experts (1,572.8 tok/s at B=128). Real multi-session workloads diverge and touch 83–112 experts per layer—still scaling cleanly from 31.8 → 603.8 → 1,077.4 tok/s.

Where GPU Time Actually Goes (prof-batch)

At B=64, MoE expert GEMMs (moe_gu + moe_down) eat 83.9% of every decode step while attention is just 1.7%. At B=256, MoE is 68.1% and attention/QKV start climbing.

The 256K Pivot · Why the Old 8K Analysis Failed
At short 8K contexts, the 40 sliding-window slots dominate KV memory. At 256K (262,144 tokens), the math flips upside down: SWA stays flat at 160 MiB (2%), while the 8 Full-attention slots explode to 8.00 GiB per session (98%)—8.16 GiB/instance (522 GiB for 64 sessions, 1.04 TiB for 128). Worse, engine.rs:525 had a 32-bit offset assert that literally crashed at 262,144 tokens (hard ceiling: 262,143). That forced us to stop trusting old README micro-tests and measure real 256K behavior from scratch.

Visual Explainer · Why Naive Quantization Dies & How DC-Mean + Hadamard Fixes It

Interactive · 3-Stage Walkthrough

Raw attention keys sit on a huge shared offset (μ) plus two violent outlier spikes that stretch a naive 3-bit grid until normal channels vanish into 0. Step through the two cheap linear transforms that turn spiky keys into a crisp bell curve before quantization even starts.

Rotated WHT-128 + Lloyd-Max Quantization

SWA Outer K3V2: KL 0.00405 (23/24)

Why rotation helps: raw K/V vectors have sharp outlier spikes that ruin low-bit rounding. Applying a fast Walsh-Hadamard transform (WHT_128) with random ±1 signs smears spikes evenly into a clean bell curve, so Lloyd-Max Gaussian buckets fit tightly. Even better: compressing the outer 768 tokens of all 40 SWA rings to K3V2 or K2V2 barely moves logits at all!

Sparse Top-K Page Retrieval vs. Static Centroids

73.3× Smaller · KL 0.038

Static No-RoPE centroid merging (averaging distant keys ahead of time) failed cleanly (KL 0.60–1.06) because you can't guess which exact detail a future query needs. Instead, keeping a tiny WHT-2bit-sketch index in RAM and pulling just the top 5% pages in WHT-3b (plus 1 background average) cut active Full KV to 111.7 MiB at KL 0.038 (22/24)—beating Quest 10% FP16 by 2.6× on accuracy at 7.6× less RAM.

Honest Failure · Static Centroid Merging
Even at gentle 8× merging (1,056 MiB FP16), static centroids hit KL 0.603 (15/24); at 32×–64× they collapsed to KL 1.05–1.06. Dynamic per-step page retrieval won by more than 15× on KL.

Two Hidden Goldmines: Constant DC Key Mean (24–49.6%) & Cross-Pass Predictability

Free SNR Boost

Why subtracting the average key is free math: in softmax attention, subtracting the same average key vector μ from every key shifts every dot product by the exact same constant q·μ, which cancels out cleanly (you only add −q·μ to the learned sink score). That one subtraction removes 24%–37% of all key energy (peaking at 49.6% on slot 0 head 0!) before quantization even starts. Meanwhile, across Inkling's 3 loop passes, blk.17 keys correlate at cos = +0.905 (out-of-sample linear R² = 0.742, up to 0.813 on head 5).

Act 2 · Disk Expert Offloading (bench-offload)

Bit-Exact (0.00e0) SSD Expert Offloading & Layer-Pipelined Macro-Batching

15.2 GiB of Inkling's 16.3 GiB weights are MoE experts. We built an OOM-safe offloader using F_NOCACHE reads, MADV_DONTNEED page eviction, and a fixed 1.38 GiB double-buffered staging ring—verifying bit-exact parity (max diff 0.000000e0, 16/16 argmax) against the resident GPU engine.

Mode A (Per-Step) vs. Mode C (Layer-Pipelined Macro-Batch)

992.2 tok/s · Bit-Exact

Why Mode C works: reading a layer from SSD once per small batch wastes I/O. Instead, Mode C loads Layer L once and runs M micro-batches of 256 tokens through it (B_macro = 512..4096, needing only 16–126 MiB of inter-layer FP16 state) while streaming Layer L+1 in the background. At B_macro=2048, prelude_coda hits 992.2 tok/s and all24 hits 848.8 tok/s.

Mode B: Zipf Hot/Cold Expert Cache Savings

97.4% SSD Saved @ B=1

Pinning the 32 hottest experts per offloaded layer (only 1.96 GiB RAM) eliminates 97.4% of SSD reads at B=1 (down to 208 MiB/step, 3.58× faster than full streaming) and 80.9% at B=8. At large batches (B=64), coupon-collector spread shrinks savings to 38.0%—that's where Mode C macro-batching takes over.

Honest Caveat · Warm macOS Page Cache vs. Cold NAND
In the sweep log, 15.2 GiB of GGUF pages were already warm in macOS's Unified Buffer Cache (UBC) from the earlier probe, yielding 29–50 GiB/s reads. A standalone cold F_GLOBAL_NOCACHE check measured 7.76 GiB/s (1 thread) / ~8–14 GiB/s true cold NAND. Crucially, Mode C still hides cold NAND: 7.83 GiB at 8 GiB/s takes ~980 ms, completely tucked behind B_macro=2048's 2,050 ms GPU compute!

Act 3 · Batched MoE Kernel Experiment & FP16 Conv State (bench-moe-opt)

FP16 Conv State Won Cleanly (−468 MiB); 6-Class Small-Tile MoE Lost to 4-Class

Good engineering means reporting what lost too. We tested two ideas together: shrinking the recurrent short-conv pool from FP32 to FP16, and splitting sparse 3–16 token MoE tiles into a 6-class router (route6) with zero-staging direct kernels.

route4 (Existing) vs. route6 (New) + FP16 CS_POOL Savings

Why route4 won: Apple M5 Pro loves 128-thread coalesced Q4_K weight staging into threadgroup SRAM. Breaking ragged experts into 6 separate indirect kernel launches hurt memory coalescing and added extra barrier overhead—only dn at B=32 edged ahead (1.05×), while total throughput dropped 5–8%. Meanwhile, FP16 CS_POOL halved conv state RAM (saving 468 MiB at B=256) at rel_L2 = 0.000098.

Kept · FP16 CS_POOL
Cut per-instance conv state from 3.83 → 1.83 MiB (−117 MiB @ B=64, −468 MiB @ B=256) with numerical error rel_L2 = 0.000098. Adopted permanently in the 256K engine.
Rejected · 6-Class MoE Router (route6)
Lost across the board: 1,155.2 → 1,098.7 tok/s at B=256 (0.95×), 532.3 → 490.9 at B=64 (0.92×). We kept the faster, simpler route4 path.
Act 4 · Multi-Agent KV Sharing (kv-share-probe)

TokenDance + KVCOMM on Zero-RoPE Inkling: 64×–256× Sharing & 1.28× Faster Attention

What if 64 agents with completely different system prompts and tool histories all read the same codebase? On RoPE models, shifting a shared document by 500 tokens breaks its KV keys. On Inkling 2 Mini, Zero RoPE + 40 SWA turns cross-prefix reuse into a cheat code.

Why Zero RoPE Makes Blocks Plug-and-Play (Plain English)
When Agent 0 reads a 3,965-token dossier after a 137-token prefix, and Agent 4 reads the exact same dossier after a 725-token prefix (+588 tokens shifted), normal LLMs have baked different rotary angles into every key. Inkling stores pure content vectors with zero RoPE—and 40 of its 48 layers can't even look back past 1,024 tokens. So pasting Agent 0's Master block straight into Agents 1–4 with 0% recompute already matches 95 of 96 next tokens (KL 0.0081)!

Visual Explainer · Why Zero-RoPE Makes Blocks Snap Together Like LEGO Bricks

Standard RoPE bakes a clock-hand angle into every cached key, so sliding a file behind a new prompt breaks every arrow. Inkling stores pure content keys and only adds relative distance bias live inside the final 1,024 tokens—so compiled blocks drop in anywhere with zero re-rotation.

TokenDance Diff Sweep: Check-Layer (blk.5) vs. Oracle

96/96 Argmax @ 2% Diff

blk.5 is the very first Full-attention layer (6 of 48 slots in). Using it as an early filter to spot and selectively recompute just 2%–10% of drifted tokens drives error down to KL 0.0049–0.0083 while cutting 64-agent 256K memory from 522 GiB down to 8–60 GiB.

Fused GPU Attention: k_attn_master_diff_b (B=64, L=4096)

1.28× Faster & 60× Less RAM

Sharing a single read-only Master block doesn't just save RAM—it runs faster on GPU. All 64 agents stream the same 136 MiB Master copy hot out of Apple Silicon's L2/SLC cache instead of thrashing 8.19 GiB of separate copies across DRAM.

KVCOMM Online Anchor-Pool on Held-Out Agent 4 (725-tok Private Prefix)

24/24 Argmax Across All Modes

Key Scientific Discovery · Quantization + Sparse Paging Are 100% Lossless (25/25); Blind Multi-File Stitching Isolates One Clear Boundary

        

          1. Kv256k-Sparse hit a perfect 25/25 (100.0%) with CoT — tying Exact FP16 on every single task! Why? During CoT, every reasoning hop updates the query and runs a fresh 1.6 ms index-card scan that pulls in the exact next page in the chain.

          2. Real source code (5 distinct Rust files, 34.4K tok) works great with blind IKV-K3V2 (seam=0): 14/15 (93.3%) in BOTH runs (with CoT: 5/5 Single-Hop, 4/5 Multi-Hop, 5/5 Code Logic) at 29.7× TTFT / 41.1× prefill speedup (20.58 s → 0.69 s) and 5.86× smaller disk.

          3. Homogenous synthetic logs cleanly exposed the exact limit of blind seam=0 packing: Because Kv256k-Sparse uses the exact same WHT-K3V2 codec and scored 25/25, quantization wasn't the cause of IKV-K3V2's 2/10 on RULER with CoT. The verbatim answers caught it red-handed: on B06 and B09, the model explicitly said "The other two cluster log files were not included"—because packing 3 nearly identical-template logs standalone from pos=0 broke sequential cross-file scanning during iterative CoT. Fix direction: add a tiny file-manifest prefix or 2–5% TokenDance check-layer refresh at stitch time when mounting homogenous logs.
